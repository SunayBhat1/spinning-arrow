A man sets out on a winter trail in the Yukon with a dog. He is a newcomer to the region and has little experience of the severity of its cold. He plans to reach a camp where other men are waiting, taking a route along a creek. The day is clear but sunless, and the temperature is far below freezing. He notices the physical effects of the cold without fully considering what they could mean for his survival. He expects to reach camp in the evening, eat with the others, and rest beside a fire.

An older man at Sulphur Creek has warned him against traveling alone when the temperature is below fifty degrees under zero. The traveler remembers the advice but has not followed it. He feels capable of managing the journey and considers some of the older man's warnings excessive. The dog experiences the conditions differently. Its instincts draw it toward shelter and fire rather than continued travel. The relationship between the two is practical rather than affectionate: the dog follows the man, expects food and warmth from him, and responds to his commands.

The trail crosses areas where water still flows under the snow and ice. Some stretches of apparently firm ground conceal pools fed by springs. Getting wet in this temperature would require an immediate halt and a fire. The traveler watches for signs of these traps and at one point sends the dog ahead to test a suspicious place. The dog breaks through and gets its feet wet. It bites ice from between its toes, while the man helps remove some of it. The episode shows the danger along the creek, but the journey continues.

When he stops for lunch, the man finds that his fingers become numb very quickly after he removes his mittens. Ice around his mouth also makes eating difficult. He lights a fire, thaws enough to eat, and experiences the relief of warmth. The dog is reluctant to leave this stopping place. The man resumes walking, still expecting to complete the trip. He is aware that the temperature is extremely low, but until this point he has been able to respond to each immediate difficulty with ordinary movements and the equipment he carries.

Later he breaks through a concealed patch of ice and wets his feet and lower legs. He must stop and dry them before proceeding. He gathers fuel and builds a fire beneath a spruce tree, where twigs are conveniently available. With a fire burning, he again thinks that his situation can be managed. He has wet clothing and boots to attend to, but he has produced warmth. As he pulls branches from the tree, however, accumulated snow shifts above him. A mass of snow falls onto the fire and extinguishes it.

The man recognizes that he has made a serious mistake by building beneath the tree. His wet feet are freezing, and his hands are losing their ability to manipulate small objects. He tries to establish a new fire in a safer place. Handling tinder and individual matches becomes increasingly difficult. At one point he lights a bundle of matches together, accepting that they burn his flesh because he needs the flame. His impaired hands interfere with arranging the fuel, and his efforts do not produce a sustainable fire. Time spent trying has left him colder.

The dog remains close, waiting for a fire that will make it comfortable again. The man's attention turns to it as he considers his own failing circulation and inability to use his fingers. He remembers a story about a man caught in a blizzard who killed a steer, crawled inside its body, and survived. The narrative stops at this recollection. It has not yet described what he will attempt with the dog, whether his hands will permit it, or whether any remaining course will succeed. The distance to camp and his deteriorating physical condition remain part of the immediate situation.
