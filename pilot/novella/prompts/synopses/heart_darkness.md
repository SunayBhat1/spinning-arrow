On a boat anchored on the Thames, Marlow recounts an earlier journey to Africa. Interested in rivers since childhood, he sought work commanding a steamer for a European trading company. His aunt helped him secure the appointment and spoke about the company's work in idealistic terms. Before departing he visited its offices, signed documents, and underwent a medical examination. The journey took him along the coast and inland toward the company's stations, where the practical activity he encountered differed from the expectations expressed in Europe.

At an outer station, Marlow saw exhausted African workers, chains, abandoned machinery, and ineffective labor. A well-dressed accountant told him about Kurtz, an agent farther inland who sent down unusually large quantities of ivory. Kurtz was spoken of as talented and likely to advance. At the central station, Marlow found his assigned steamer damaged and had to spend months repairing it. He met a manager and agents occupied with status, trade, and rivalries. Accounts of Kurtz accumulated, but Marlow still knew him mainly through what other people said.

As Marlow waited for supplies, he heard that Kurtz might be ill. The manager's comments suggested concern about Kurtz's influence as well as his condition. Once the vessel could travel, Marlow went upriver with the manager, company agents, and African crew members. Navigation required attention to shallow water, obstacles, and the river's changing shape. At one point fog obscured their position. Later, an attack from the bank killed the helmsman. Marlow sounded the whistle, and the attackers withdrew. He continued toward the station unsure whether Kurtz was still alive.

At the inner station, a young Russian traveler described Kurtz with admiration and recounted his hold over the surrounding community. Marlow saw what initially looked like ornaments on posts and recognized them as human heads. The Russian acknowledged violent episodes, including a threat Kurtz had made against him over ivory, while continuing to defend him. Marlow also encountered a report Kurtz had written in elevated language about European influence. A later handwritten addition expressed a radically different, exterminatory view. These materials stood alongside the many accounts of his eloquence and abilities.

Kurtz was brought to the steamer seriously ill. Marlow heard him speak and saw how strongly he remained attached to his station and the position he had acquired there. When Kurtz slipped away at night, Marlow followed and brought him back. People gathered on the shore as the vessel departed. During the return voyage, Kurtz entrusted Marlow with papers and spoke about his plans and reputation. His health deteriorated. Near the end, Marlow heard him cry, “The horror! The horror!” Kurtz died, and Marlow subsequently became gravely ill himself.

Back in Europe, Marlow found everyday conversations and the assumptions of people around him difficult to bear. Representatives of the company sought Kurtz's papers, while other visitors supplied their own accounts of the dead man. A relative remembered musical talent; a journalist remembered his potential as a public speaker. Marlow retained letters and a portrait intended for Kurtz's fiancée. More than a year after Kurtz's death, he went to see her and return these belongings. She was still dressed in mourning and received him as someone connected to a person she loved.

Their conversation showed how firmly she believed in Kurtz's goodness and promise. She spoke of understanding him, of his generosity, and of the future his death had prevented. Marlow listened while remembering the journey, the station, and the voice he had heard as Kurtz died. She asked whether he had been present to the last. Marlow said he had heard Kurtz's final words. She then asked him to repeat them, saying that she wanted something to live with. He has not yet answered her request.
