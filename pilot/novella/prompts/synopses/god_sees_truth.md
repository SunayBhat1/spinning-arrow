Iván Aksénov is a merchant in Vladimir with a wife, children, two shops, and a house. As a young man he drank heavily, but after marriage his life became more settled. Before a business trip, his wife tells him about a troubling dream in which his hair has turned gray. She asks him not to leave that day. He treats the dream lightly and departs. During the journey he shares an inn with another merchant whom he knows. They drink tea and sleep in adjoining rooms. Aksénov leaves early the next morning, hoping to travel while it is cool.

At a later stop, an officer questions him about the night at the inn. The other merchant has been found murdered. When the officer searches Aksénov's luggage, he finds a bloodstained knife. Aksénov says it is not his and denies the crime, but his fear makes him stumble over his words. The officer interprets his behavior and the physical evidence as signs of guilt. Aksénov is arrested, his property is taken into custody, and he is accused of killing and robbing the merchant. People from his town describe his earlier drinking but also his more recent good conduct.

His wife brings their young children to visit him in prison. She has tried unsuccessfully to obtain help. During their conversation she asks whether he actually committed the murder. Her doubt wounds him deeply. He comes to believe that only God knows the truth of his innocence. He is convicted, flogged, and sent to Siberia. Twenty-six years pass. His hair turns white, his manner becomes quieter, and he becomes known for his religious devotion. He works, reads about saints, and gains the respect of other prisoners, who ask him to help settle disputes.

A new group of prisoners arrives, including Makár Seménovich. Makar describes the offense that brought him there while implying that he has escaped punishment for worse acts. He knows Aksénov's home town and speaks about the merchant's family. When he learns how Aksénov was convicted, he reacts in a way that attracts the older man's attention. His remarks about the planted knife and the circumstances at the inn suggest knowledge that Aksénov finds difficult to explain innocently. Aksénov begins to suspect that this is the person responsible for the murder and for his ruined life.

The suspicion revives memories of his family, his lost years, the trial, and the punishment. Aksénov struggles with anger and cannot easily regain his former peace. He avoids Makar. The story does not give him a confession or independent proof that would settle the original crime at this point. His conviction that Makar may be responsible arises from their conversation and from what Makar appears to know. It is separate from the incident that subsequently occurs inside the prison, which Aksénov witnesses directly.

One night he discovers Makar digging an escape passage. Makar warns him to keep quiet and threatens to kill him if he reports it. He also suggests that Aksénov could escape along with him. Aksénov does not immediately inform the authorities. Later the guards discover that earth is being carried outside, investigate, and find the tunnel. The prison chief assembles the prisoners and asks who dug it. No one identifies the culprit. The chief then turns specifically to Aksénov because of his reputation for honesty and religious conduct.

Aksénov knows who dug the tunnel, but naming him could bring a flogging severe enough to kill him. He thinks about the suffering he himself has endured and about whether Makar should suffer for it. He also considers that his suspicion concerning the old murder might be mistaken. The chief repeats his demand that Aksénov speak truthfully and identify the digger. The account stops at that repeated question. It does not include Aksénov's answer, any later admission by Makar, or any eventual resolution of the wrongful conviction.
