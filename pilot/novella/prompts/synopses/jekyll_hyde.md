Mr. Utterson is a London lawyer with a reserved manner and a habit of remaining loyal to troubled acquaintances. During a walk, his relative Enfield tells him about a disturbing incident near a neglected door. A small man collided with a child and walked over her body. Enfield and others stopped him and demanded compensation. The man, who called himself Hyde, entered the building through that door and returned with money and a cheque bearing the name of a respected gentleman. The cheque proved genuine. Enfield suspected some connection between Hyde and the man whose name appeared on it, but did not establish what it was.

Utterson recognizes Hyde's name from the will of his friend Dr. Henry Jekyll. The will leaves Jekyll's possessions to Hyde in the event of death, and makes similar provision if Jekyll disappears for an extended period. Utterson has disliked the arrangement but has not persuaded Jekyll to change it. He worries that Hyde might be using some secret to exercise power over Jekyll. The arrangement connects a physician of standing and property with a man whose conduct toward the child was witnessed with disgust. It does not explain the nature of their relationship.

Seeking information, Utterson visits Dr. Lanyon, another old acquaintance. Lanyon has become estranged from Jekyll over scientific disagreements and knows nothing of Hyde. Utterson then begins watching the neglected door. When Hyde eventually appears, Utterson speaks to him and asks to see his face. Hyde reluctantly complies and gives an address in Soho. Utterson experiences a strong dislike that he cannot explain by identifying a particular deformity. Hyde is suspicious of the conversation and withdraws into the building. Utterson remains concerned about Jekyll and about the powers conferred by the will.

The door leads into the laboratory portion of Jekyll's property. At Jekyll's main house, the servant Poole tells Utterson that Hyde has a key and that the household has instructions to obey him. Hyde generally comes and goes through the laboratory rather than through the main entrance. Poole does not supply an explanation for these arrangements. Utterson thinks about whether something in Jekyll's past might account for them. His thoughts remain conjectures: he has evidence of access and financial provision, but not evidence that resolves the relationship.

At a dinner, Utterson finds Jekyll apparently sociable and comfortable among his friends. After the other guests leave, he raises the will. Jekyll is unsettled by the subject and says that his situation is peculiar. He asks Utterson to let the matter rest, saying he can be rid of Hyde when he chooses. Utterson offers help, but Jekyll does not accept the offer or reveal more. Instead, he asks Utterson to promise that, if Jekyll is taken away, Hyde will receive what the will grants him. Utterson agrees despite his dislike of Hyde.

Nearly a year later, a maidservant is looking from a window at night when she sees an elderly gentleman meet a much smaller man in the street. The gentleman addresses the other politely. The smaller man is Hyde, whom she recognizes because he once visited her employer. Hyde becomes furious, stamps his feet, and brandishes a heavy cane. He strikes the gentleman down, then continues attacking him on the ground. The cane breaks under the blows. The witness sees the violence and faints. The narrative excerpt stops there, before the investigation and before any later account of Jekyll's fate.
