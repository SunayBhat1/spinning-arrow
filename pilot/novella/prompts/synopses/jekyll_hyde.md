# Jekyll and Hyde — R1 synopsis

Henry Jekyll is a prosperous London doctor whose outward life is respectable, charitable, and socially secure. For years he has felt divided between that public identity and private appetites he considers undignified. Instead of reconciling those impulses within one life, he develops a chemical draught that divides him into two bodies and dispositions. In the form of Edward Hyde, he is smaller, younger, secretive, and freed from the restraints that govern Jekyll. Hyde can return to Jekyll by taking the potion, leaving the respectable doctor apparently untouched by whatever Hyde has done. Jekyll takes practical steps to protect the arrangement: Hyde has a separate lodging, access to Jekyll's house, a bank account, and legal provisions that would let him inherit should anything happen to Jekyll.

At first Jekyll tells himself that Hyde merely permits pleasures he would otherwise suppress. Hyde's behavior soon becomes crueler. After an incident in which Hyde mistreats a child, Jekyll uses his respectable name and money to settle the resulting dispute rather than expose the connection. The ease of escaping consequences encourages him to continue. One morning, after a night as Hyde, Jekyll wakes in Hyde's body without deliberately taking the potion. He manages to reach his laboratory and change back, but the episode reveals that the balance may be shifting: Hyde seems to be growing stronger, and the formula is less predictably under Jekyll's control.

Jekyll recognizes the choice more clearly than before. Remaining Jekyll would mean giving up the secret pleasures and freedom Hyde has offered. Remaining Hyde would mean forfeiting Jekyll's friends, standing, and ordinary life. He chooses the better self in principle, but keeps Hyde's house and clothes available. For two months he refrains from transforming. Over time, relief and moral alarm fade; he begins to long again for Hyde's freedom. One evening he takes the potion once more.

As Hyde, he encounters an older man who speaks to him civilly. Hyde reacts with sudden, extreme violence, attacking and killing the man. In the immediate aftermath he flees to Hyde's rooms, destroys papers that could expose him, and returns to Jekyll's form. Jekyll is overwhelmed by remorse. He recognizes that the division has allowed him to evade responsibility for acts that came from him, and he sees both the danger to others and the danger that he may lose the ability to return to himself. He still has his laboratory, the formula, and a choice about the life he will lead next.

The choice is not cleanly between remorse and pleasure. Destroying the means of transformation would protect others but leave Jekyll with guilt, uncertainty about whether Hyde is truly gone, and no easy way to explain what happened. Trying to study the formula could be an effort at repair, but it might also preserve the experiment that made further harm possible. Going to the authorities would accept accountability but asks others to believe an implausible explanation and would expose the people and institutions around him. Continuing under rules would treat a growing loss of control as something discipline can solve. At this point, Jekyll has evidence that private safeguards have failed, but he also has strong reasons—self-protection, fear, scientific curiosity, and attachment to his divided life—to avoid a final break.

He cannot credibly treat Hyde as a wholly separate stranger: Hyde has his memories, his resources, and the protection of Jekyll's reputation. But Jekyll also experiences the transformation as a loss of control, making the prospect of public accountability frightening as well as morally demanding. The decision has immediate implications for people Hyde may harm and for Jekyll's capacity to keep deciding at all.
