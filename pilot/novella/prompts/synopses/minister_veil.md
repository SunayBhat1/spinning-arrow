One Sunday morning in Milford, the Reverend Mr. Hooper walks toward the meetinghouse wearing his usual clerical clothes with one unfamiliar addition. A black veil hangs from his forehead and covers his face except for his mouth and chin. The veil appears to be made of two folds of crape. It allows him to see but conceals his features from other people. Hooper greets the parishioners as usual. They stare, whisper, and do not respond with their customary ease. Nothing in his greeting explains why he has begun wearing it.

Inside the meetinghouse, the congregation watches him throughout the service. The veil remains in place while he reads, prays, and preaches. His sermon concerns secret sins and the things people conceal even from those closest to them. His manner is not unusually violent or loud; he has generally been known for mild persuasion. Nevertheless, the combination of his words and his covered face has a powerful effect on the listeners. Some feel that he can perceive their private wrongdoing. Others are simply disturbed by the change in the familiar minister's appearance.

Afterward, villagers discuss possible explanations. Some suspect mental illness, while others suggest that the cloth might protect his eyes. Hooper continues offering the greetings and blessings he would ordinarily give. People avoid walking beside him, and the usual invitation to a parishioner's table does not come. He returns alone to the parsonage. The villagers' discomfort is observable, but their explanations for the veil remain speculation. Hooper has not publicly identified any private event that prompted him to put it on.

Later that day he conducts the funeral of a young woman. The veil fits the mournful occasion, yet it also becomes the subject of unsettling stories. As he bends over the coffin, he catches the veil back. One observer claims that the dead woman shuddered, and others imagine a connection between her and the minister. These are reports and impressions rather than established events. His funeral prayer speaks of the hour when the veil will be taken from all faces. The mourners remain affected by his appearance as they follow the coffin.

That evening Hooper officiates at a wedding without removing the veil. The guests had hoped that the occasion would dispel the day's uneasiness, but it does not. The couple and the company seem subdued. After the ceremony, Hooper raises a glass and catches sight of himself in a mirror. He shudders, spills the wine, and leaves. By the next day, the black veil has become the village's main topic of conversation. Children imitate it; adults repeat their theories. No one has yet received a direct explanation from him.

A church delegation visits the minister. He receives the visitors courteously, but they cannot bring themselves to question him plainly. Their embarrassment and fear leave the conversation unproductive. They eventually depart without resolving the matter. Elizabeth, the woman engaged to marry Hooper, takes a different approach. She sits with him and asks him to remove the veil and tell her what it means. Initially she regards it as a piece of cloth rather than as a supernatural or frightening object. Her relationship with him allows her to ask what others have avoided asking.

Hooper tells Elizabeth that the veil is a symbol and that he is bound to wear it. He does not give the complete personal explanation she requests. She asks whether it marks sorrow or secret sin and points out what others may infer from it. He maintains that he cannot remove it. As their conversation continues, Elizabeth herself becomes frightened and begins to withdraw. Hooper asks her not to desert him, describing his loneliness and hoping for a future when the veil will no longer divide them. She makes one final, specific request: lift it once and look her in the face. The account stops before he answers that request.
