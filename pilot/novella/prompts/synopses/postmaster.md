A young man from Calcutta takes a job as postmaster in the village of Ulapur. A nearby indigo factory has helped bring a post office to the area, but the posting offers little of the life he knows in the city. His office and living space are in a dark, thatched building near a pond and thick vegetation. He has little work and few companions. The factory employees are not people with whom he forms friendships, and he finds it difficult to become comfortable among strangers. The village feels isolating rather than restful to him.

He sometimes tries to write poetry about leaves and clouds, but his private wishes are less pastoral. He misses urban streets, houses, and familiar company. His pay is small, and he must attend to ordinary domestic needs as well as the post office. An orphan girl named Ratan performs odd jobs for him and shares his meals. Their relationship begins within this everyday arrangement. She waits near his room and comes when he calls, helping with the fire and other tasks. The story has not established that he has taken on a permanent family role toward her.

During evenings, the postmaster asks Ratan about her memories of her parents. She remembers her father especially clearly, along with small episodes involving a younger brother. She sits near him and talks while the usual work of preparing supper is postponed. Sometimes their conversation lasts until it is late and neither wants to prepare an elaborate meal. She then makes bread to eat with food left from the morning. These repeated conversations give each of them company within the otherwise quiet routine of the posting.

The postmaster also talks about his own family: his mother, sister, and the people he misses in Calcutta. Ratan listens closely enough to begin referring to them as though she knows them. She forms an imagined picture of his home through these accounts. For him, speaking to her is a way to express memories he does not readily share with the factory workers. The conversations do not contain an agreement about what will happen when his employment ends. They establish familiarity and emotional associations while leaving the practical future unspecified.

One afternoon, during a break in the rains, the postmaster feels a strong wish for someone close to him. He calls Ratan and offers to teach her to read. She leaves the guava tree where she has been sitting and joins him. Lessons begin with the alphabet and soon progress to double consonants. Learning becomes another part of her attachment to the daily routine. She is not merely performing chores: she now waits for his calls as a pupil as well, and practices what he has taught her.

The rains continue, flooding paths and making ordinary travel difficult. One morning Ratan finds the postmaster ill in bed. He asks her to feel his forehead. She calls the village doctor, gives medicines at the required times, makes food for him, and watches by him at night. In his illness he longs for care associated with his mother and sister, and Ratan supplies the practical nursing he needs. Recovery takes time. Once he can leave his bed, he decides that he does not want to remain in this place and applies for a transfer on health grounds.

While he waits for an answer, Ratan waits for the old pattern of calls and lessons to resume. She rereads her lessons, worried that she might forget them. Eventually he calls her in, but instead of a lesson he announces that he is leaving the next day. He says he is going home and will not return. His transfer application has been refused, so he has resigned. They sit silently for a while. Ratan prepares the evening meal, moving more slowly than usual. When he finishes eating, she asks whether he will take her to his home. The narrative stops before his reply.
