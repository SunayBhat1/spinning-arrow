Between nine and ten on a September evening, Dr. Kirilov's only son, Andrey, dies of diphtheria. The boy is six years old. His mother sinks beside the bed in grief. The household's servants have been sent away because of the illness, so the doctor himself answers a ring at the door. He has not put on his coat or wiped his face and hands. His hands bear the effects of the disinfectant used during the child's illness. The interruption comes only minutes after the death, while the household is still absorbed in what has happened.

The visitor is Abogin, who says they have met before. He is pale, breathless, and visibly agitated. He tells Kirilov that his wife has suddenly become dangerously ill and that a carriage is waiting outside. According to his account, they had been entertaining an acquaintance when she cried out, clutched at her heart, and collapsed. He tried ammonia and water, but she remained unresponsive. He fears an aneurysm because her father died of one. This is information supplied by the visitor; Kirilov has not examined the woman or independently confirmed her condition.

At first Kirilov listens without responding as though he has not absorbed the request. When Abogin repeats himself, the doctor explains that his son died five minutes earlier and that he cannot come. Abogin is shocked and hesitates. He acknowledges the terrible timing but says he does not know where else to turn. He insists there is no other doctor nearby and emphasizes that he is asking for a patient rather than seeking help for himself. His distress appears sincere, though he continues to press after hearing about the death.

Kirilov turns away and walks through the house. His movements are unsteady and mechanical. He pauses over ordinary objects and appears disoriented, as if the rooms were unfamiliar. In the bedroom, the child lies motionless with his eyes open. Kirilov's wife kneels beside him, her head in the bedclothes. Medicines, bowls, and other traces of the struggle with the illness remain around the room. The doctor stands looking at his son. His expression is subdued, but tears are visible in his beard. He then moves through other rooms before returning to the passage.

Abogin has stayed by the door. Seeing the doctor again, he assumes that he is ready to leave. Kirilov repeats that he cannot go. Abogin says that he understands the doctor's sorrow but is not requesting a routine consultation: he believes a life is in immediate danger. He appeals to courage, professional duty, and humanity. Kirilov objects that humanity applies to his own family too. He says he can hardly stand, is not fit to do anything, and cannot leave his wife alone at this moment.

The exchange becomes more strained. Kirilov refers to a regulation that obliges him to attend and says bitterly that Abogin could drag him away if he wished. Abogin rejects that formulation. He says he has no right to force him and is appealing to his feelings, not asserting control over his will. He suggests that someone who has just suffered a loss should understand his fear for his wife. The doctor and the visitor are both expressing distress, but neither appeal has yet brought them to a settled course of action.

After further pleading, Kirilov asks how far away Abogin lives. Abogin answers that it is approximately eight or nine miles. He says his horses are excellent and gives his word that the doctor can go there and return within an hour. His last assurance is that it will be only one hour. The narrative stops at that promise, before Kirilov accepts or rejects the request. There is no information here about what a visit would reveal, whether the promised duration is realistic, or what happens to either household afterward.
