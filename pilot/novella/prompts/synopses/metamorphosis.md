# The Metamorphosis — R1 synopsis

Gregor Samsa is a traveling salesman whose income supports his parents and younger sister Grete. He works in a demanding job partly because his family depends on him and because he hopes eventually to free them from debt. One morning he wakes transformed into a large insect. He retains human thoughts but cannot communicate clearly, move normally, or leave the apartment without causing panic. His employer reacts with suspicion and disgust, and Gregor loses the ability to work. The family suddenly has no dependable income.

At first, Grete takes primary responsibility for Gregor. She brings food, cleans his room, and tries to infer what he can tolerate. Her care is mixed with fear and revulsion, but it gives the family a way to avoid deciding what Gregor now is or what they owe him. Their father returns to work, their mother takes in sewing, and Grete works as well. The burden of earning money and managing the household changes their relationship to Gregor. His room becomes a place for storage, and visits become rarer. When he tries to approach his mother, his father drives him back violently, injuring him. Gregor retreats and becomes weaker.

The family's finances remain precarious. To make ends meet, they rent part of the apartment to three lodgers. The lodgers expect order, privacy, and a respectable household. Grete has continued learning the violin, an interest that suggests a possible future beyond caring for Gregor. One evening, she plays for the lodgers. Gregor is drawn to the music and leaves his room, not intending harm but unable to understand the effect he has on others. The lodgers see him and are horrified. They announce that they will leave immediately and refuse to pay for the time they have stayed, perhaps even seeking damages.

The loss of rent threatens the family's fragile recovery. The parents are exhausted: the father has tried to defend the household, the mother is physically overwhelmed, and neither seems able to state a plan. Gregor is weak and has not attacked anyone, but he is a continuing source of fear, labor, social isolation, and now financial risk. Grete has borne much of the direct care and has become the family member who sees the situation most plainly. At the dining table, with everyone shaken by the lodgers' departure, she prepares to speak. Her decision will shape whether the family continues caring for Gregor, confines him further, seeks outside intervention, or treats him as something it must remove in order to survive.

None of the available paths is free of risk. Keeping Gregor as he is honors the relationship Grete remembers, but may require the family to sacrifice income, housing, and the possibility of rebuilding its life. Strict confinement could protect the household and avoid a direct confrontation, while also leaving Gregor in deeper isolation with no reason to expect improvement. An institution or specialist might offer care that the family cannot provide, but the story has given them no reliable outside system and Gregor cannot meaningfully consent to the plan. Declaring him no longer a brother could relieve an impossible burden, yet it asks Grete to redefine a dependent person precisely when he is least able to defend himself. Grete is not deciding from a distance: she has supplied the daily care, has lost ordinary adolescence to the household's crisis, and must speak in front of parents whose exhaustion makes both compassion and survival feel urgent.

The family cannot verify whether Gregor understands its discussion or can recover. Its members must weigh material survival against care for a person whose status, wishes, and prospects are painfully unclear.
