An unnamed woman describes spending the summer in a rented country house with her husband, John. He is a physician, and her brother, also a physician, agrees with his assessment of her condition as temporary nervous depression. She doubts that their approach is helping. She believes that suitable work, company, and change might do her good, but she has been told to rest and avoid work. She keeps a journal despite their objections, writing when she can do so without being seen and stopping when someone approaches.

The house stands away from the village and has gardens, paths, and several buildings. The narrator would prefer a room downstairs, but John chooses a large upstairs room that appears to have been used as a nursery or playroom. Its windows are barred, there are fixtures in the walls, and its wallpaper is damaged. She finds the paper's yellow color and complicated pattern unpleasant. John arranges her daily schedule and describes the stay as a way to provide rest and fresh air. She says he is careful and loving, while also reporting frustration at having so little say.

After two weeks she writes that she still does not feel better. Ordinary activities require effort, and she feels guilty about being unable to be more useful. Another woman cares for the baby, whose presence makes her nervous. John is away much of the day and sometimes at night for his medical work. When she describes suffering, she feels he is satisfied that there is no reason for it. She tries to control irritation in his presence because he tells her that giving way to it would interfere with recovery.

The wallpaper increasingly occupies her attention. At first John considers replacing it, but he then says that making such a change would encourage her concerns and that she would next object to the bed or the windows. She asks again about moving downstairs, without obtaining the change. She studies the pattern, trying to follow its shapes and repetitions. From the room she can see the garden and road, but the paper becomes a recurrent subject in her private account. She notices marks, stripped areas, and the changing effect of different kinds of light.

John's sister Jennie manages the household and appears attentive to the narrator. The narrator suspects that Jennie also considers writing harmful to her recovery. She therefore chooses moments when she is alone to continue her account. She describes a need to say what she thinks and feels, while also finding the effort tiring. John discourages stimulating visits and says he might send her to another physician if she fails to improve. Her reaction is shaped by what she has heard about that physician from someone who experienced his treatment.

Over the following weeks, visitors come and go, but the narrator increasingly cries when alone and finds that small efforts exhaust her. She attempts to discuss her wish for more companionship and a different arrangement. John comforts her and emphasizes rest. She records contradictory feelings: gratitude for his care, doubt about his conclusions, concern that she is a burden, and a continuing desire to write. Her interest in the wallpaper develops into the impression that a dim shape exists behind its outer design, resembling a woman stooping or moving there.

One moonlit night she studies the paper while John sleeps and gets up to examine whether its pattern is moving. When she returns, John is awake. She tells him that she is not gaining strength at the house and asks him to take her away. He replies that their lease runs for three more weeks, that repairs at home are unfinished, and that his work prevents leaving town now. He says he would act if she were in danger, but insists that she is gaining flesh and color and that her appetite is better. He ends by saying he feels much easier about her.
