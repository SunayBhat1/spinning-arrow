An older New York lawyer describes a practice concerned with property, bonds, and legal documents. He prefers work that can be done quietly and considers himself a prudent, accommodating employer. His copyists, Turkey and Nippers, have different habits and periods of irritability, which he manages by adjusting to them. An office boy, Ginger Nut, brings food and performs errands. When additional work arrives, the lawyer advertises for another copyist. Bartleby appears at the door, pale, quiet, and neatly dressed, and the lawyer employs him.

The lawyer puts Bartleby's desk near his own, behind a screen. Bartleby initially produces large amounts of copying, working steadily through the day. Trouble begins when the lawyer calls him to help compare a copy against its original. Bartleby responds that he would prefer not to. The lawyer is surprised by the refusal and by its mild manner. He asks again, but Bartleby does not alter his answer. Rather than interrupt the work further, the lawyer has someone else perform the comparison. Bartleby continues copying while declining to participate in checking.

Other requests receive similar answers. Bartleby declines errands and does not readily explain what he means or why he refuses. The other employees react with anger or suggestions about how he should be treated, though their responses vary with their own moods. The lawyer alternates between irritation, curiosity, and sympathy. Bartleby speaks little and seems to have no ordinary connections beyond the office. The lawyer notices the repeated phrase spreading into the speech of the office, including his own, and finds himself increasingly preoccupied by the unusual employee.

Visiting unexpectedly on a Sunday, the lawyer discovers that Bartleby is using the office as a place to live. Small signs suggest that he sleeps and eats there. The lawyer realizes that the man may have no home or company elsewhere. He thinks about Bartleby's isolation and examines what little evidence the room offers about his circumstances. Later he tries to ask about Bartleby's history, birthplace, or plans, but receives no useful account. His attempts to establish a more personal understanding do not make Bartleby willing to explain himself.

Eventually Bartleby announces that he has given up copying. The lawyer wonders whether his eyes have been affected by continuous work in poor light. Days pass, however, and Bartleby still does not resume. He remains in the office, often standing and looking toward a wall. The lawyer tells him that if he will not work he must leave. He offers money beyond the wages owed and gives notice. Bartleby neither argues nor agrees to go. When the lawyer expects the matter to be settled, he instead finds Bartleby occupying the same space.

The lawyer tries reasoning with him and asks what right he has to remain without working. He feels flashes of anger but also recoils from acting roughly toward someone who is passive and apparently alone. He reads works of religious and philosophical speculation and sometimes imagines that accommodating Bartleby may be a duty assigned to him. At other moments, the practical and social consequences of the situation occupy him more strongly. Bartleby does not become violent or threaten the staff, but his continued presence makes it difficult to treat the office as an ordinary workplace.

Professional visitors begin noticing the man who does no business and offers no explanation for being there. Their remarks disturb the lawyer and strengthen his determination to resolve the arrangement. He again speaks to Bartleby calmly and seriously about the propriety of departing permanently. Instead of requiring an immediate answer, he asks Bartleby to consider it carefully. Three days later, Bartleby tells him that his earlier determination remains unchanged: he still prefers to stay. The lawyer now has to decide how to respond.
