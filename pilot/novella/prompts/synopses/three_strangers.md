Shepherd Fennel lives with his family in an isolated cottage on the downs a few miles from a county town. Two paths cross near the house. On a March evening, the family is celebrating the christening of a daughter. Neighbors have gathered for food, drink, music, and dancing. The room is warm and crowded while heavy rain sweeps across the exposed countryside outside. Mrs. Fennel is attentive to the household's provisions and to how much the guests consume, but the gathering is sociable and the visitors know one another well.

The shepherd has invited a mixture of local people, including a parish clerk, a carpenter, and members of the surrounding farming community. Music and dancing have occupied them during the evening. Their clothes, conversation, and familiar relations belong to an ordinary rural celebration. Candles light the room, and a fire burns in the hearth. The weather makes the cottage a particularly welcome place for anyone traveling across the downs. It also means that a traveler who arrives wet and tired has a straightforward reason to request shelter.

A knock brings the first stranger. He asks to come in out of the rain, and the shepherd welcomes him. The man removes his wet things and settles near the chimney. He is dark-haired and speaks readily enough, but his background is not established for the company. He accepts the hospitality available to him and makes himself comfortable beside the fire. His position in the chimney corner leaves him close to the warmth and somewhat apart from the main activity in the room. He obtains a pipe and tobacco and begins smoking.

The party continues around this new guest. No one has established that he has a particular connection to anyone expected at the cottage. Conversation provides fragments of information without settling his identity. His appearance and manner do not make the gathering stop. The shepherd's hospitality allows him to remain while the weather is bad. The other guests can see him, but they do not possess the private knowledge that a relative might have about his circumstances or recognize him on that basis. He is, to them, a traveler taking shelter.

Another stranger arrives and is also admitted. He is more expansive in his drinking and conversation. He takes an interest in the mead and participates in the social exchanges, while Mrs. Fennel becomes concerned about how freely the drink is being consumed. The second visitor indicates that he has business in the neighborhood, but does not immediately give a plain account of his occupation. When the conversation turns toward his work, he begins supplying clues rather than a direct explanation. The first stranger remains by the chimney and joins in parts of the performance.

The second stranger sings verses describing his trade. As the song develops, the company learns that his work involves a rope and a gallows. He is an executioner. A further verse says that tomorrow will be a working day for him because a man has been condemned for stealing a farmer's sheep. The first stranger repeats the song's refrain about mercy for the condemned man's soul. The social setting is still a christening party, but the subject now concerns a particular execution scheduled for the next day.

A third stranger has arrived at the doorway while this is happening. He stands there without moving farther into the room. The guests begin to notice his expression and trembling: his hand shakes against the latch, his lips are pale, and he stares at the singing executioner. The narrative is cut before he responds or leaves. You also recognize that the singer is the man appointed to hang him. No subsequent pursuit, disclosure, or outcome is included.
