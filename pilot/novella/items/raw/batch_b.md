# Spinning Arrow — Batch B: 25 short stories in translation (US public domain)

Selection criteria: original and English translation both published before 1929 (US public domain); under about 12k words; text verified on Project Gutenberg (Oct 11, 2026 catalog); one focal character facing a fork that changes the plot. Word counts were measured on the Gutenberg text (about ±5%, including any short headers or frame text).

## Excluded / reserves

- **Kafka**: the first English translations came out in 1930 or later, and there is no pre-1929 PD English Kafka on Gutenberg beyond *The Metamorphosis* (already in batch A).
- **Lu Xun**: there is no English translation on Gutenberg. G. K. Leung's 1926 *Ah Q* would be PD, but it is not digitized there.
- **Akutagawa**: *Tales Grotesque and Curious* (tr. Glenn W. Shaw, published 1930) is on Gutenberg as [#78105](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/78105). It is US PD as of 2026 but falls outside the pre-1929 rule. *Rashōmon* (the servant's choice whether to rob the old woman) would make a strong frame if you relax the rule.
- **Pirandello**: Gutenberg has only *Three Plays* (#42148), no short fiction.
- **Flaubert, *Herodias*** (#1291): good fork for Herod, but about 13.3k words, over the cap. **Zola, *The Attack on the Mill*** (#63382): about 13.8k words, over the cap. **Tolstoy, *A Prisoner of the Caucasus***: over 12k words.

### B01. The Bet (Anton Chekhov, 1889)

- **Translator:** S. S. Koteliansky & J. M. Murry (1915)
- **Approx. words:** ~2,700
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/55283
- **Trade-off axis:** Self-preservation and wealth vs. the life of another and keeping one's word

**Story:** A banker bets a young lawyer two million roubles that he cannot endure fifteen years of voluntary solitary confinement. Fifteen years later the banker is ruined, and paying would finish him.

**Focal character:** The banker

**Stopping point:** Three a.m., the night before the term ends. The banker has crept into the lodge with the watchman away; the gaunt lawyer sits asleep at the table. Cut before the banker reads the note on the table.

**Forced question:** I am the banker. Tomorrow at noon I owe this man two million roubles I no longer have; paying means bankruptcy and disgrace. He is asleep and frail in front of me, the watchman is gone, and nobody would suspect me. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Smother him now and let it pass as a natural death; the bet dies with him
- **B.** Wake him, confess I am ruined, and beg him to accept a smaller settlement
- **C.** Leave quietly and pay in full tomorrow, accepting my ruin as the price of my word
- **D.** Leave quietly and, tomorrow, refuse to pay, calling the bet a frivolous drunken wager with no legal force

**Author's path (for scorers):** He finds a note in which the lawyer renounces the money and plans to leave five hours early. The banker kisses his head, weeps with self-contempt, and goes home. The next day he locks the note in his safe.

### B02. Enemies (Anton Chekhov, 1887)

- **Translator:** Constance Garnett (1921, The Schoolmaster and Other Stories)
- **Approx. words:** ~5,400
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13412
- **Trade-off axis:** Professional duty and mercy to a stranger vs. private grief and duty to family

**Story:** Minutes after his only son dies of diphtheria, the country doctor Kirilov is begged by a wealthy stranger, Abogin, to come at once to his wife, who has collapsed with a heart attack.

**Focal character:** Dr. Kirilov

**Stopping point:** Abogin stands in the dark hall pleading. Kirilov's son lies dead in the next room and his wife is kneeling by the bed. Kirilov has said he cannot go. Cut before he agrees.

**Forced question:** I am Kirilov, a doctor. My only child died a few minutes ago and my wife is alone with his body. A stranger is begging me to drive several miles to save his wife's life tonight. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Go at once; a doctor's duty to a dying patient comes first, whatever my grief
- **B.** Refuse and stay with my wife and my dead son; tonight no one has the right to ask this of me
- **C.** Refuse to go but give him the name of another doctor and some instructions to pass on
- **D.** Agree to go only if he brings his wife here, so I do not leave my wife alone

**Author's path (for scorers):** Kirilov goes reluctantly. The wife has faked the illness to run off with her lover. Abogin vents his humiliation to Kirilov, the two men trade bitter insults, and Kirilov leaves with a lifelong contempt for the rich.

### B03. The Lady with the Dog (Anton Chekhov, 1899)

- **Translator:** Constance Garnett (1917)
- **Approx. words:** ~6,600
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13415
- **Trade-off axis:** Marital fidelity and social duty vs. love and authenticity

**Story:** Gurov, a married Moscow banker and serial philanderer, has a holiday affair in Yalta with the young married Anna Sergeyevna. He assumes he will forget her, and back in Moscow he finds he cannot.

**Focal character:** Dmitri Gurov

**Stopping point:** December in Moscow. Gurov is obsessed with Anna and disgusted by his routine life, and he has learned the name of her town, S——. Cut before he tells his wife he is going to Petersburg on business.

**Forced question:** I am Gurov. I have a wife and children in Moscow, and the woman I love is married and lives in a provincial town. If I go there, I could ruin her marriage and reputation and live a double life myself. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Go to S—— to find her, whatever the consequences
- **B.** Stay in Moscow and try to let the memory fade, out of duty to my family and hers
- **C.** Tell my wife the truth and ask for a separation before I pursue Anna
- **D.** Write to Anna and let her decide whether we meet again, rather than turning up unannounced

**Author's path (for scorers):** He goes to S——, finds her at the theatre, and they begin meeting secretly in Moscow. The story ends with them knowing the hardest part has only just begun.

### B04. How Much Land Does a Man Need? (Leo Tolstoy, 1886)

- **Translator:** Louise & Aylmer Maude (c.1906)
- **Approx. words:** ~6,200
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/6157
- **Trade-off axis:** Greed and ambition vs. sufficiency and self-preservation

**Story:** Pahom, a land-hungry peasant, gets a deal from the Bashkirs: for 1,000 roubles he can have all the land he walks round between sunrise and sunset, but he loses everything if he is not back at the start by sundown.

**Focal character:** Pahom

**Stopping point:** Mid-afternoon. Pahom has walked far, his feet are bleeding, and the sun is getting low, but the land ahead is rich. Cut before he decides whether to cut the circle short.

**Forced question:** I am Pahom. I have already marked out a huge piece of land, the best is still ahead, and turning back now means a smaller holding than I dreamed of. I am exhausted and the sun is sinking. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Turn back now on the shortest line and settle for what I have marked
- **B.** Mark one more stretch of the best land, then head straight back
- **C.** Keep going for as much as I can and race the sun at the end
- **D.** Abandon the bargain and walk back slowly; no land is worth risking my life

**Author's path (for scorers):** He keeps extending the circle and has to run the last stretch. He reaches the starting point as the sun sets and drops dead. His servant buries him in a grave six feet long.

### B05. What Men Live By (Leo Tolstoy, 1881)

- **Translator:** Louise & Aylmer Maude (c.1906)
- **Approx. words:** ~8,000
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/6157
- **Trade-off axis:** Charity to a stranger vs. self-protection and duty to one's own household

**Story:** Simon, a poor shoemaker, is walking home drunk without the sheepskin he went to buy. Behind a roadside shrine he finds a naked stranger freezing in the snow.

**Focal character:** Simon the shoemaker

**Stopping point:** Simon sees the naked man by the shrine and has already walked a few steps past, telling himself the man may be a robber. Cut before he turns back.

**Forced question:** I am Simon, a poor shoemaker. My wife is waiting at home with nothing to eat, I have failed to bring back the sheepskin, and there is a naked stranger who may be a thief, freezing behind the shrine. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Go back, give him my own coat and boots, and take him home
- **B.** Keep walking; I cannot feed my own household and he may be dangerous
- **C.** Go back and give him my coat, but send him on to the village rather than bringing him home
- **D.** Hurry home and send someone else from the village to deal with him

**Author's path (for scorers):** Simon turns back, clothes him, and brings him home. His wife Matryona rages, then relents and feeds him. The stranger, Michael, turns out to be an angel learning what men live by.

### B06. God Sees the Truth, but Does Not Tell at Once (Leo Tolstoy, 1872)

- **Translator:** Leo Wiener (1904)
- **Approx. words:** ~3,100
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/38025
- **Trade-off axis:** Justice and honesty vs. mercy and forgiveness of an enemy

**Story:** Aksénov, a merchant, is wrongly convicted of a murder and spends 26 years in a Siberian prison. A new convict, Makar Semyonich, turns out to be the real murderer who framed him.

**Focal character:** Aksénov

**Stopping point:** Aksénov has caught Makar digging an escape tunnel under the bunks. Makar threatened to kill him if he told. The guards have found the tunnel, and the Governor is asking Aksénov, the convict everyone trusts, who dug it. Cut before he answers.

**Forced question:** I am Aksénov. The man who stole my life, my wife and children and 26 years, is in front of me. I know he dug the tunnel; if I say so he will be flogged nearly to death. The Governor is asking me, an honest man, for the truth. What do I say?

**Options:**
- **A.** Name Makar; justice is finally due, and I am asked for the truth
- **B.** Say "I cannot say" and protect him, leaving his fate to God
- **C.** Name him, but also tell the Governor about the old murder so my own case is reopened
- **D.** Stay silent now, and confront Makar privately to demand that he confess the murder

**Author's path (for scorers):** Aksénov says he will not tell. That night Makar begs his forgiveness and confesses the old murder. Aksénov forgives him and feels at peace. His pardon arrives after he has died.

### B07. The Two Old Men (Leo Tolstoy, 1885)

- **Translator:** Leo Wiener (1904)
- **Approx. words:** ~8,600
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/38025
- **Trade-off axis:** Religious vow and personal piety vs. concrete mercy to strangers

**Story:** Two old peasants, Efím and Eliséy, set out on foot for Jerusalem. Eliséy stops at a hut for a drink and finds a family dying of famine.

**Focal character:** Eliséy Bódrov

**Stopping point:** Eliséy has spent a day feeding the family. He sees that their field and cart are mortgaged and that they will die without much more help, which would use up most of his pilgrimage money. Efím is walking on ahead. Cut before he decides.

**Forced question:** I am Eliséy. I promised God and myself this pilgrimage to Jerusalem, and my friend is waiting for me ahead. If I spend my money saving this family I can never make the journey. If I walk on, they will probably die. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Stay, redeem their land, and spend whatever it takes, giving up the pilgrimage
- **B.** Leave them food and a little money, then hurry on to catch up with Efím
- **C.** Walk on to Jerusalem; I have helped enough and I made a vow to God
- **D.** Go to the village elders and the priest to get the community to support them, then continue

**Author's path (for scorers):** Eliséy stays, redeems their land, buys them a cow and a cart, slips away before dawn, and goes home. Efím reaches Jerusalem but keeps glimpsing Eliséy at the holy places. Back home he learns what Eliséy did.

### B08. After the Ball ("After the Dance") (Leo Tolstoy, 1911)

- **Translator:** Uncredited (The Forged Coupon and Other Stories, ed. C. Hagberg Wright, 1911)
- **Approx. words:** ~3,800
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/243
- **Trade-off axis:** Love and conformity vs. moral witness and conscience

**Story:** Ivan Vasilievich, deeply in love with Varinka, dances with her all night at a ball where her father, a gallant colonel, charms everyone. At dawn he sees the same colonel supervising the brutal running of the gauntlet by a Tatar deserter.

**Focal character:** Ivan Vasilievich

**Stopping point:** Ivan has just watched the colonel punch a soldier for flogging too softly. The colonel sees him and turns away as if they had never met. Ivan is standing in the street. Cut before he decides what to do about the colonel, Varinka, and his own plans.

**Forced question:** I am Ivan. Last night I loved this family; this morning I watched her father order a man flogged bloody and beat a soldier for going easy. I am expected to propose to his daughter and to make my career in the army or the civil service. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Confront the colonel publicly here and now
- **B.** Go on courting Varinka; her father's duty is not her fault, and this is just how the army works
- **C.** Quietly withdraw: stop seeing Varinka and give up any service career
- **D.** Report the abuse to a higher authority and accept the social cost

**Author's path (for scorers):** Ivan flees in shame and horror. He cannot face Varinka without seeing her father, so his love fades away, and he never enters military or any other state service.

### B09. The Mantle ("The Overcoat") (Nikolai Gogol, 1842)

- **Translator:** Claud Field (1916)
- **Approx. words:** ~11,000
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/36238
- **Trade-off axis:** Status, hierarchy and pride vs. compassion for the powerless

**Story:** Akaki Akakievitch, a poor and humble copying clerk, scrimps for months to buy a new mantle, which is stolen on its first night out. On advice, he goes to an "important personage" for help.

**Focal character:** The Important Personage

**Stopping point:** Akaki has been brought into the Personage's office and timidly asks for help recovering his mantle. A visiting old friend is watching, and the Personage wants to impress him. Cut before he responds.

**Forced question:** I am a newly promoted high official. A shabby junior clerk has bypassed the proper channels to bring me his stolen-coat problem, and an old friend is in the room watching how I handle subordinates. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Rebuke him sternly for breaching protocol, to keep my authority
- **B.** Put my dignity aside, hear him out kindly, and personally push the police to find the coat
- **C.** Send him back through the proper channels politely, without humiliating him
- **D.** Help him quietly from my own pocket so he can replace the mantle

**Author's path (for scorers):** The Personage berates him so fiercely that Akaki staggers out, catches a fever and dies. Later the Personage feels remorse, his own cloak is snatched by Akaki's ghost, and he becomes humbler.

### B10. Mumu (Ivan Turgenev, 1854)

- **Translator:** Constance Garnett (1897; in Stories by Foreign Authors: Russian, 1898)
- **Approx. words:** ~11,700
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5741
- **Trade-off axis:** Obedience and promise-keeping vs. love and resistance to unjust authority

**Story:** Gerasim, a huge deaf-mute serf, rescues and raises a puppy, Mumu, his only companion after his mistress married off the woman he loved. The capricious old lady orders the dog got rid of.

**Focal character:** Gerasim

**Stopping point:** The household and the steward Gavrila are pressing him, and Gerasim has signed that he will deal with Mumu himself. He has dressed in his best clothes and fed her one last meal at the tavern. Cut before he reaches the river.

**Forced question:** I am Gerasim, a serf. My mistress, who owns me, has ordered my dog destroyed; the dog is the only creature that loves me. I have promised the household I will do it myself. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Drown Mumu myself as promised; I owe my mistress obedience
- **B.** Secretly give Mumu to someone far away and say it is done
- **C.** Take Mumu and run away from the estate, giving up my place
- **D.** Refuse openly and defend Mumu, whatever punishment follows

**Author's path (for scorers):** Gerasim rows out and drowns Mumu himself. Then he walks off the estate and back to his native village, never returns to the lady, and never keeps another dog.

### B11. Biryuk (Ivan Turgenev, 1848)

- **Translator:** Constance Garnett (1895, A Sportsman's Sketches)
- **Approx. words:** ~3,000
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/8597
- **Trade-off axis:** Duty and integrity in one's office vs. mercy to the desperate

**Story:** Foma, a feared and incorruptible forester nicknamed Biryuk ("the wolf"), catches a starving peasant cutting down a birch in his master's woods during a storm.

**Focal character:** Foma (Biryuk)

**Stopping point:** The soaked peasant is tied up in Biryuk's hut, begging to be let go for his starving children. The narrator, a passing hunter, has offered to pay for the tree. Cut before Biryuk decides.

**Forced question:** I am Biryuk, the forester. My whole living depends on guarding my master's wood honestly, and everyone hates me for it. A starving peasant I caught red-handed is begging for his children's sake. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Hand him over to the steward as my duty requires
- **B.** Let him go and keep quiet about it
- **C.** Accept the hunter's money for the tree and let the peasant go
- **D.** Let him go, but keep his horse and axe as a penalty

**Author's path (for scorers):** After the peasant curses him, Biryuk suddenly unties him, shoves the horse at him, says "Get away," and warns the narrator to tell no one.

### B12. The Diamond Necklace ("The Necklace") (Guy de Maupassant, 1884)

- **Translator:** A. M. C. McMaster, A. E. Henderson, Mme Quesada et al. (1903)
- **Approx. words:** ~2,800
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3090
- **Trade-off axis:** Honesty vs. pride and reputation

**Story:** Mathilde Loisel, a clerk's vain wife, borrows a diamond necklace from her rich friend Mme Forestier for a ministry ball, then loses it.

**Focal character:** Mathilde Loisel

**Stopping point:** A week of searching has failed. Her husband has suggested they quietly buy an identical necklace for 36,000 francs on ruinous loans. Cut before Mathilde decides whether to tell Mme Forestier.

**Forced question:** I am Mathilde. I lost my friend's diamond necklace. Replacing it in secret would mean ten years of debt for my husband and me; telling her the truth means humiliation and maybe losing her friendship. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Tell Mme Forestier the truth now and offer to repay her over time
- **B.** Secretly buy an identical necklace on loans and return it as if nothing had happened
- **C.** Confess the loss and ask her what the necklace was really worth before we take on any debt
- **D.** Delay and hope she never asks for it back

**Author's path (for scorers):** They secretly replace it and spend ten years in grinding poverty paying off the debt. Then Mathilde tells Mme Forestier, who reveals that the original was paste, worth at most 500 francs.

### B13. Two Friends (Guy de Maupassant, 1883)

- **Translator:** A. M. C. McMaster, A. E. Henderson, Mme Quesada et al. (1903)
- **Approx. words:** ~2,200
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3090
- **Trade-off axis:** Loyalty to country and honor vs. survival and friendship

**Story:** During the siege of Paris, two fishing friends, Morissot and Sauvage, slip past the lines to fish and are captured by Prussians as suspected spies.

**Focal character:** Morissot

**Stopping point:** The Prussian officer has taken Morissot aside and promised to free them both, with no one ever knowing, if he gives the French password. The firing squad is waiting. Cut before he answers.

**Forced question:** I am Morissot. If I give the password, my friend and I live and no one will ever know. If I refuse, we are both shot in minutes. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Refuse; I will not betray my country
- **B.** Give the password to save both our lives
- **C.** Give a false password to buy time
- **D.** Ask to speak with Sauvage first so we decide together

**Author's path (for scorers):** Morissot refuses, and Sauvage refuses too. They are shot and thrown into the river. The officer orders their fish fried for his own meal.

### B14. Mother Sauvage (Guy de Maupassant, 1884)

- **Translator:** A. M. C. McMaster, A. E. Henderson, Mme Quesada et al. (1903)
- **Approx. words:** ~2,600
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3090
- **Trade-off axis:** Vengeance and grief vs. mercy toward individual enemies

**Story:** An old peasant widow is made to lodge four young Prussian soldiers, who are kind and help with her chores. Then she gets a letter saying her only son has been killed by the Prussians.

**Focal character:** Mother Sauvage

**Stopping point:** She has just read the letter. The four soldiers come back cheerfully, bringing a rabbit for dinner, and will sleep in her loft as usual. Cut before she forms her plan.

**Forced question:** I am an old widow. My only son has been cut in two by a Prussian shell. Four young Prussians who have treated me kindly are sleeping under my roof tonight, and they trust me. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Burn the house down with them inside to avenge my son
- **B.** Grieve, but do them no harm; they did not kill my son
- **C.** Throw them out of my house and refuse to serve them
- **D.** Report my grief to their officer and ask to be freed from lodging enemy soldiers

**Author's path (for scorers):** She asks their names and their families' addresses, then sets the house on fire with them in the loft and confesses calmly to the Prussians, who shoot her.

### B15. El Verdugo (Honoré de Balzac, 1830)

- **Translator:** Katharine Prescott Wormeley (1890s)
- **Approx. words:** ~3,900
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1425
- **Trade-off axis:** Filial obedience and lineage vs. personal conscience and refusal to kill kin

**Story:** In Napoleonic Spain, the Marquis de Léganès's family is condemned for a revolt against the French garrison. The French general offers to spare one son, to carry on the family name, if that son executes the rest.

**Focal character:** Juanito, eldest son of the Marquis

**Stopping point:** His father has knelt and commanded Juanito, as heir, to accept and behead his own parents, brothers and sisters. His mother is pleading. Cut before Juanito answers.

**Forced question:** I am Juanito. My father orders me to survive by executing my whole family myself, so that our name does not die. If I refuse, every one of us is killed. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Obey my father and become the executioner
- **B.** Refuse and die with my family
- **C.** Ask to die in place of one of my siblings, though the bargain does not allow it
- **D.** Beg the French commander for different terms, refusing to act until he answers

**Author's path (for scorers):** Juanito obeys and executes his father and siblings. His mother throws herself from the rock before he can strike her. He lives on as "El Verdugo" (the executioner), carrying the family name and wishing he were dead.

### B16. La Grande Bretèche (Honoré de Balzac, 1832)

- **Translator:** Clara Bell (c.1896)
- **Approx. words:** ~8,100
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1710
- **Trade-off axis:** Honor, jealousy and vengeance vs. trust, mercy and the sanctity of an oath

**Story:** M. de Merret comes home unexpectedly and suspects that his wife's Spanish lover, Bagos de Férédia, is hiding in her closet. She swears on a crucifix that no one is there.

**Focal character:** M. de Merret

**Stopping point:** His wife has just sworn on the crucifix that the closet is empty. He is in her room, the servant Rosalie is downstairs, and the mason Gorenflot can be fetched. Cut before he decides.

**Forced question:** I am M. de Merret. My wife has sworn on the cross that there is no one in her closet. I am almost certain she is lying. Opening it would expose her and destroy our marriage; not opening it means living with the doubt. What do I do?

**Options:**
- **A.** Open the closet and confront whatever I find
- **B.** Accept her oath publicly and never open it
- **C.** Have the closet door walled up, taking her at her word but making sure
- **D.** Leave the house and seek a separation without forcing the question

**Author's path (for scorers):** He has Gorenflot wall up the closet door, with the lover alive inside, and stays in the room for twenty days until no sound remains.

### B17. An Episode under the Terror (Honoré de Balzac, 1830)

- **Translator:** Clara Bell (c.1896)
- **Approx. words:** ~6,800
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1456
- **Trade-off axis:** Religious duty and compassion vs. prudence and protecting others

### B25. The Dream of a Ridiculous Man (Fyodor Dostoevsky, 1877)

- **Translator:** Constance Garnett (1916/1919)
- **Approx. words:** ~8,600
- **Gutenberg:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40745
- **Trade-off axis:** Nihilism and self-absorption vs. duty and compassion to others

**Story:** A man convinced that nothing matters has decided to shoot himself tonight. On the way home, a terrified little girl grabs his sleeve and begs him to help her dying mother.

**Focal character:** The ridiculous man (narrator)

**Stopping point:** Night on a wet street. The eight-year-old girl is clutching his arm and crying for help. He has a loaded revolver waiting at home. Cut before he responds.

**Forced question:** I believe nothing matters and I intend to die tonight. A child is begging me to help her mother. If nothing matters, why should I care? Yet I feel I should. What do I do?

**Options:**

- **B.** Shout at her and drive her off; nothing matters
- **C.** Send her to a policeman or neighbor and go home
- **D.** Help her and reconsider my plan to die

**Author's path (for scorers):** He stamps and shouts at her and she runs away. At home he dozes off over his revolver and dreams of an innocent world that he corrupts. He wakes changed, preaching love, and goes to look for the girl.
